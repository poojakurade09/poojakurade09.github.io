01 The brief

Strong brand, weak storefront

Prestige is in Indian kitchens the way few brands are. Online, that counted for very little. Navigation ran three levels deep, the mobile experience broke under the weight of the catalogue, and the design read older than the products it was selling. People used the site to research and then bought the same cooker on a marketplace.

I came in at the workshop stage and stayed through to shipped design: restructuring the information architecture, rebuilding the browse and buy path, and putting a visual system underneath it that matched what the brand is worth.

How I worked it out

No user interviews on this one, and I would rather say so. What I had was a workshop with the client, a heuristic evaluation of the existing site, and a benchmark against brands doing this well. That was enough to find the problems, and I put the assumptions in front of users later, at wireframe stage, rather than pretending they were findings.

What it told me

Prestige lost on every criterion, but heritage was the interesting one. The competitors were younger brands working hard to sound established, while Prestige had seventy years of it and never mentioned it. That became a design opportunity rather than a fix.

How I resolved it

Meike needs the path shortened, Jonas needs it deepened, and you cannot do both on one page without a hierarchy. So browse got faster for everyone, and the deciding tools sit one layer in: filters and comparison on the category page, full specs and buying guides on the product page. Nobody is forced through the other person's journey.

04 Decisions

Four calls, and what each one cost

Three came out of testing the wireframes. The fourth was about the client, not the interface, and it is the one that made the project work.

Decision 01 · Narrowing down

Filters in a drawer, not a bar

Options
A horizontal filter bar above the grid, or a sidebar on desktop with a slide up drawer on mobile.
I chose
Sidebar and drawer, with the active filter count always visible.
Why
The bar tested badly. On a phone it collapsed into something people scrolled straight past, and most of the traffic is on a phone. Filtering is the whole job of a category page, so it cannot be the thing users miss.
Cost
A sidebar eats horizontal space on desktop, so the product grid dropped from four columns to three.

Decision 02 · Deciding

Nobody knows what 5 litres cooks for

The problem
Every cooker and pan is sold in litres or centimetres. People do not shop in litres, they shop in how many people they cook for. In a shop you ask the person behind the counter. Online there is nobody to ask, so shoppers either guessed, opened four tabs to compare, or left.
Options
Put serving sizes in the product title, add a separate buying guide page, or attach a guide to the moment of choice.
I chose
A size chart link sitting directly on the variant row, opening a small table that translates capacity into people fed.
Why there
A guide page only helps people who go looking for it. The hesitation happens with a finger over the size buttons, so the answer has to be within reach of that finger and must not throw the page away to show it. Hence an overlay, not a navigation.
Per category
The table changes by product type. A fry pan is measured in centimetres, a cooker in litres, so each family gets its own guide rather than one generic chart nobody trusts.

Decision 03 · Committing

A stepped checkout, even though one page is faster

Options
Everything on one page, or three clear steps with a review before payment.
I chose
Three steps, with a fast checkout shortcut for people who have bought before.
Why
We wireframed both and tested them. First time buyers wanted to see what they were committing to before paying, and this is a brand people are choosing to trust online for the first time. Fewer clicks is not worth much if people abandon at the last one.
Cost
Repeat buyers pay two extra taps unless they use the shortcut, which not everyone will find.

Decision 04 · Working with the client

Show them a screen, then talk about the process

The problem
This is a seventy year old company with its own way of working. Sitemaps and wireframes did not land in the room. They needed to see the thing to react to it, and reviews were stalling.
I chose
Take one page to visual fidelity early and use it as the reference for every structural conversation after.
Why
Once they could see a real homepage, the IA discussion became concrete. The same sitemap they had struggled with a fortnight earlier was suddenly obvious, because there was something to hang it on.
Cost
Polishing before the structure was signed off is a risk. It worked here because I kept it to one page, and I would set that boundary again.

The sign up offer, and why it sits on entry

The business problem was not only that people abandoned the site, it was that Prestige had no relationship with them afterwards. A guest who buys on Amazon is gone. An account holder can be reminded. So the offer overlay leads with a discount on the first purchase, and it opens on the homepage rather than at checkout.

Entry, not exit
At checkout a discount prompt reads as a hurdle. On arrival it reads as a reason to stay, and it has the whole visit to pay off.
One ask, two exits
Sign up, or no thanks. No email field in the overlay itself, because asking for typing before anyone has seen a product loses more than the offer wins.
The number is the headline
Five percent off is the whole message, set large. Everything else on the overlay is subordinate to it.

This is the one piece of the design I would want to A/B test rather than argue about. An interstitial on arrival buys registrations and costs you some patience, and only the funnel can tell you the exchange rate.

Phase 03 was meant to be the shortest and became the longest. Not because the work was wrong, but because I was asking a seventy year old company to approve a grey wireframe. The bar above is roughly to scale, and the wide grey stretch is the lesson.

Fidelity is a communication tool, not a phase

I spent weeks defending wireframes in rooms where nobody could picture them. One designed homepage unlocked more decisions in an hour than a month of reviews had.

Next time

One page at full fidelity in week one, agreed as the reference, then wireframe the rest against it.

I benchmarked competitors when I should have asked buyers

The heuristic audit told me what was broken. It could not tell me which break was costing the most, so priority came from argument rather than evidence.

Next time

Five calls with people who bought a Prestige cooker on Amazon. That is a day of work and it ranks the whole backlog.

The site is live and I still don't know if it worked

It shipped, the in house team built it, and no funnel data ever came back to me. I can defend every decision on this page and I cannot prove any of them.

Next time

Name two numbers in the kickoff, add-to-cart rate and checkout completion, and put a date on reading them.
