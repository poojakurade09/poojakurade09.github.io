01 The brief

Find out why the current flow was failing

Duplicate files, empty metadata, abandoned folders, no naming convention, and nobody whose job it was to hold any of it together. Teams had built their own systems around it and stopped going back.

I audited the current state end to end, mapped how assets actually moved, and handed back a prioritised plan.

What I did

Interviews told me what people believed. The audit told me what was actually in the library. Where the two disagreed was where the problems were.

Stakeholder interviews Content audit Process mapping Workshop facilitation Prioritisation mapping
Search could not work, because nothing was tagged

Folders named by campaign, team, date and person, sometimes all four in one path. With metadata empty, results fell back to upload date.

Content audit and interviews agreed
Assets went in and never came out

No review, no expiry, no archive policy. Files years past relevance sat beside live campaign work.

No lifecycle defined anywhere
The platform was nobody's job

IT ran the infrastructure, marketing used it, nobody owned governance. Every symptom traced back here.

The root cause, not a symptom
02 Who I spoke to

Three relationships to the same platform

Three groups, three different ideas of what a working platform means. The person who runs it, the people who put things in, the people who need things out.

Runs the platform
Platform owner
Six years in role, holds the whole library in her head

"I spend a large part of my week answering where is the file. I should not be a human search engine."

Her memory was the only working search the organisation had, which is why nothing improved.
Puts assets in
Asset creator
Creative lead, agency side, uploads after each campaign

"I upload files after campaigns but I have no idea what happens next. Half the time someone remakes an asset I already made."

Uploading ended his job and started nobody else's. That gap made the duplicates.
Needs assets out
Asset consumer
Marketing operations, global team, shares assets daily

"I never know if I am sharing the latest approved version. I email the creative team directly. The platform is a last resort."

His risk was reputational, not convenience, so he chose the slow certain route every time.
What that told me

All three had a workaround that worked. Nobody was fighting the platform, they had quietly routed around it, so the real system of record had moved into inboxes and memory.

04 Decisions

Three calls, and what each one cost

On a research engagement the decisions are about method and framing rather than layout. These are the three I would defend.

Decision 01 · Method

Audit the library, not just the people

Options
Interviews alone, which is faster and what was expected, or interviews plus a full content audit.
I chose
Both, and I ran the audit before I finished analysing the interviews.
Why
People describe the workaround they built, not the system that made them build it. The audit gave me evidence nobody could argue with, and in a room full of stakeholders who each had a theory, that mattered more than another quote.
Cost
The audit ate a large share of the timeline, so I ran fewer interviews than I would have liked and leaned on internal referrals to recruit.
Decision 02 · Framing

Name it an ownership problem, not a search problem

Tension
Everyone asked for better search. Search was genuinely broken. It was also the symptom furthest from the cause.
I chose
Lead the recommendation with governance and ownership, and show search as downstream of it.
Why
Renaming folders fixes search until the next quarter of uploads arrives. Without an owner and a rule at upload, the chaos regenerates. The technology could already do what they needed, it had just never been set up to.
Cost
It is the harder sell. An ownership recommendation asks for headcount and behaviour change instead of a feature, so I had to pair it with quick wins people could feel within weeks.
Decision 03 · Handover

Let them prioritise it, in the room

Options
Deliver a finished roadmap, or bring the findings and let the team place the actions themselves.
I chose
A facilitated workshop where they mapped every action onto impact against effort.
Why
I was leaving and they were implementing. A plan they built is a plan they defend when it competes with other work. It also surfaced constraints my interviews had missed, because people say different things in front of their peers.
Cost
You give up control of the outcome. Two things I would have ranked higher went to deprioritise, and I let them, because the ranking being theirs was the point.
05 Recommendations

Four pillars that only work together

Fixing one without the others buys a few months. A convention with no owner drifts. An owner with no schema has nothing to enforce.

01
Taxonomy and naming

A structure obvious enough to follow without a manual, enforced where the file enters.

Top level folders by content type
Brand, campaign, asset type, version
Legacy folders archived, not deleted
02
Metadata schema

Fewer fields, mostly picked from a list. Make tagging faster than skipping it.

Fourteen fields down to six required
Controlled lists, not free text
Date, uploader and format automatic

07 What comes next

The state worth building towards

A plan needs a destination, not just a list of fixes. Four plain tests for whether any piece of work moves them towards it.

One route in

An asset leaves the studio and enters the library through a single handover, tagged at that moment, so the two systems stop drifting apart.

Findable by anyone

Search works for someone who was not in the campaign, because the words they would use are in the metadata rather than in a colleague's memory.

Current by default

Approved and expired are states an asset actually has, so what you find is safe to send without checking with a person first.

Owned

Somebody's name is against the platform, with the time and the mandate to keep the rest of it true a year from now.

What I took from it

Technology gets blamed for problems that are organisational. The platform could already do everything these teams needed. Nobody had ever been made responsible for setting it up that way, and four years of that compounds into something that looks like a software failure.

Finding the problems was not the hard part, the audit made those undeniable. The hard part was getting people past the quick fix, because renaming a few folders feels like progress and takes an afternoon. Making the case for the ownership work meant showing that every symptom they hated sat downstream of it.

Workshop earlier

The prioritisation session surfaced constraints that would have changed which interviews I ran and which parts of the library I audited first.

More voices, shorter sessions

Long interviews with a few people gave depth. Fifteen minute conversations with many more would have told me how widely each workaround had spread.

Agree the measures up front

I left without a baseline anyone had signed off, so nobody could prove the improvement later. I would fix that in the first week now.
