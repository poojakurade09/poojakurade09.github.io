01 / 22
The story

A cab leaves a record. An auto leaves nothing.

If you lose a bag in a cab
✓You know the driver's name
✓You have the number plate
✓There is a receipt in your email
✓You can call support
If you lose a bag in an auto
✕No name
✕No number plate
✕No receipt
✕Nobody to call

An auto ride is quick because nothing is recorded. You raise a hand, agree a fare, get out. That speed is the whole appeal, and it is also the reason a forgotten bag is simply gone.

A cab you can trace. An auto you can only remember.

The framing I started from
02 / 22
Problem
Problem statement

A passenger who leaves something in an auto has no driver, no record and no place to report it. A driver who finds something has nobody to return it to.

Both sides want the same outcome. Nothing connects them. That gap is the whole product.

Why it is worth solving

The bag is rarely the real loss. A work laptop means company data. A folder means identity documents, and replacing those starts with filing a police complaint to prove they are gone. Recovering the item in the first hour avoids all of it.

03 / 22
Challenge

Three constraints I set myself

01
Reporting a find must take seconds

A driver has a minute between fares and no reason to spend it on this. If reporting a found bag means downloading an app, making an account or typing, they will leave the bag where it is. So: no sign up, no typing, nothing to learn.

02
Works with no ride data at all

There is no booking to look up. The only inputs are a fuzzy memory and an approximate place.

03
Safe to use for both people

A public list of valuables and locations is a shopping list for the wrong person. It cannot be open.

04 / 22
Research

What I would need to find out

Read this before the next four sections

I have not run formal interviews. What I do have is why I started: I have watched colleagues leave a work laptop in an auto and realise it minutes later, with nothing to go on. The panic is not really about the laptop, it is the documents inside it, and the police complaint you now have to file to prove you lost them.

So the personas and pain points below come from watching this happen, not from a research plan. I am marking that clearly rather than dressing observation up as findings, because the difference matters more than the page looking complete.

If this became real, this is the research I would run first, in this order, because each answer changes the product more than the one after it.

Would a driver ever open this?

Intercept interviews at stands. If the answer is no, the QR mechanic is the entire product and the app is irrelevant.

Highest risk
How do people describe a lost object?

Ask twenty people to describe something they lost. Their words become the matching vocabulary, not mine.

Shapes the AI
What happens to found items today?

Auto unions, police stations and stand leaders already have informal systems. Any real version has to sit on top of those, not replace them.

05 / 22
Personas

Two users, opposite tolerances for effort

The design decision lives in the gap between them. One is highly motivated and will do whatever it takes. The other is doing me a favour.

P
The passenger
Smartphone native, high urgency

Just realised something is gone. Will type, photograph, verify, refresh, and check back every ten minutes. Effort is not the barrier here, hope is.

Design implication
Give this person depth. Detail, status, notifications, a sense that something is happening.
D
The driver
Low friction tolerance, working

Found a bag between fares. Willing to help, unwilling to sign up for anything. Every extra screen is a reason to leave the bag where it is and forget about it.

Design implication
No account, no app install, no typing. Four taps and done.
A framing I deliberately avoided

It would be easy to write the driver as "not educated" and stop there. That is a stereotype, and worse, it produces no design. "Low friction tolerance while working" is both more accurate and immediately actionable: it tells me to cut steps, not to simplify language.

Wants to return it, has no idea to whom

Step 05
Nothing happens

Item sits in an auto or a stand, owner gives up

Two adjacent steps both fail for the same reason, and it is not a lack of goodwill. Both people are trying. There is simply no channel between them, so the product does not need to persuade anyone, only to connect them.

Pain points

Four things in the way

No identity attached to the ride
Nothing to look up afterwards, so recovery starts from zero every time.
Memory is vague, forms are precise
People remember "somewhere near Linking Road, maybe four o'clock". No dropdown accepts that.
Reporting a find costs the finder
Time, data, and the risk of being accused of taking something. Doing nothing is safer.
Anyone can claim anything
Without proof of ownership, a public lost and found becomes a way to collect other people's things.

Opportunities

Three places to intervene

Give the ride a trace

A QR sticker inside the auto is the cheapest possible identity. No hardware, no app, no data plan. It just has to be there.

Accept vague input

If the system can read a sentence, the passenger does not have to translate their memory into a form. This is the one place AI earns its place.

Verify by detail, not by document

The owner knows what is inside. That knowledge is the proof, and it never has to be shown publicly.

The AI

One job: turn a sentence into a search

AI is not the product here, it removes one specific obstacle. A person who has just lost something cannot fill in eight fields, but they can say what happened. The model's only task is turning that into structure the register can match on.

What the person says
"I left a black shopping bag in an auto around Linking Road. It had a red blouse, a jewellery box and two sarees."
→
What the register receives
ItemShopping bag
ColourBlack
AreaLinking Road
TransportAuto
Contents, kept private
Red blouse · jewellery box · two sarees
Why not a form

A form asks the user to guess our categories. A sentence asks nothing. The structure still exists, it just gets built on our side instead of theirs.

Language is not a barrier

Hindi, Marathi, English, or all three in one sentence, which is how people actually speak in Mumbai. Voice input matters more than typing here.

The user stays in charge

Everything extracted is shown back as editable chips. The model proposes, the person confirms. It never silently decides what you lost.

Where this design could fail

A confident wrong extraction is worse than no extraction. If it reads "red blouse" as the item rather than the contents, the match never happens and the person never knows why. That is why every field is visible and editable, and why the contents field is treated separately: it is the one used for verification later.

Visual language

Borrowed from the auto, not from Silicon Valley

A soft purple gradient app would look like it came from somewhere else. Mumbai autos are already a design system: black and yellow, painted signage, high contrast because it has to be legible in the sun. I took the palette from the vehicle.

The decision that makes it work

The QR is per auto, not per driver. It answers "which vehicle" without anyone signing up, and it survives the driver changing phones, losing the number, or never installing anything. A sticker is also something a union or a stand can hand out at scale for the price of printing.

Trust

Proving it is yours, without publishing what it is

An open list of valuables with locations and times is a gift to the wrong person. Three mechanisms keep it useful without making it exploitable.

First question people ask
If a driver posts what they found, can everyone see it?

No. A found post is never browsable. It goes into the register and is only ever surfaced to people whose own lost report already matches it. If you have not reported losing something, there is nothing for you to look at.

Nobody sees
The public has no browse or search
No map of found items
No listings page at all
A matched claimant sees
Category and colour, no more
The area, not the exact spot
A blurred photo
After verifying
The clear photo
A collection point and time
A four digit handover code
The cost of this choice: if nobody ever reports the loss, the found item sits in the register unseen. I would rather lose those cases than run a public list of where valuables were last seen.
The contents are the password

A listing shows the category and colour only. To claim it you have to name what is inside, which only the owner and the finder know.

Public sees
Black bag, Bandra
Never public
Jewellery box, two sarees
Photos are blurred until verification

Enough to recognise a shape and a colour, not enough to describe the contents back to us convincingly.

→
A handover code, not phone numbers

Four digits, exchanged at a public stand. Neither person gets the other's contact details, and both get a record that the handover happened. Anyone in India who has taken a delivery already knows this pattern.

Testing

What I would test, and what would kill it

Not yet tested

No prototype has been in front of a user. Rather than invent results, here is the test plan and the specific outcome that would make me abandon each part.

Can a driver complete the found flow unaided?

Hand a phone to ten drivers at a stand with no instruction beyond the sticker.

Kills it if
Fewer than eight finish, or anyone abandons at the camera step.
Does the extraction survive real language?

Feed it fifty real descriptions in mixed Hindi, Marathi and English and check every field by hand.

Kills it if
Confidently wrong more often than obviously blank. Wrong is worse than empty here.
Can someone game the verification?

Give five people a listing and ask them to claim an item that is not theirs.

Kills it if
Any one of them succeeds. A lost and found that can be farmed is worse than none.

The solution
Where the concept landed

A shared register with two front doors: a sentence for the person searching, a sticker and four taps for the person who found it.

The insight
Both sides already want to help. The product only has to remove the reasons not to.
The mechanic
A per auto QR gives an anonymous ride an identity for the price of a sticker.
The AI
One job. It turns how people speak into something a database can match, then gets out of the way.
22 / 22
Learnings

What a concept can and cannot prove

Designing for two tolerances is the whole job

One user will do anything, the other will do almost nothing. Averaging them produces a product that fails both. Splitting the experience was the only honest answer.

Asymmetric users need asymmetric interfaces.

The persona wording changed the design

"Not educated" would have led me to simplify words. "Low friction tolerance" led me to delete steps. Same user, completely different product.

How you describe a user is a design decision.

AI was most useful in the smallest role

I could have made this an AI product. Confining it to one translation step, from sentence to fields, is what keeps the rest of the design accountable and understandable.

Give it one job it is genuinely better at.

The limits of what this is worth

No users, no data, no proof that a single driver would scan the sticker. What this shows is how I frame a problem, choose constraints and design against them. Not that the product works.

A concept shows reasoning, never results.

The hardest constraint was not technical. It was accepting that one of my two users would never open the app, and designing as if that were fine.
